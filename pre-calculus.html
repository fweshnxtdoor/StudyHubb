UNIT 1

Conic Section

A conic section is a curve formed by the intersection of a plane and a double-napped cone (a cone with two identical parts extending in opposite directions). Depending on the angle and position of the plane relative to the cone, the intersection can produce one of four different curves: a circle, ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola. These curves are called conic sections because they are all slices or "sections" of a cone, and they have important applications in physics, astronomy, engineering, and architecture due to their unique geometric properties.

Circle

A circle is a set of all points in a plane that are at a fixed distance, called the radius, from a fixed point known as the center. It is a perfectly symmetrical, closed curve where every point on the circle is equidistant from the center. The standard equation of a circle in coordinate geometry is (x−h) ^2+(y−k) ^2=r^2, where (h, k) is the center and r is the radius

Parabolas

A parabola is a U-shaped curve formed by the set of all points in a plane that are equidistant from a fixed point called the focus and a fixed line called the directrix. It is a type of conic section that results when a plane cuts a cone parallel to one of its sides. In coordinate geometry, the standard form of a parabola with a vertical axis is y = a(x − h)^2 + k, where (h, k) is the vertex, the highest or lowest point on the graph.

Ellipses

An ellipse is a smooth, closed curve that represents the set of all points in a plane for which the sum of the distances from two fixed points, called foci, is constant. It looks like a stretched or flattened circle and is a type of conic section formed when a plane cuts through a cone at an angle, but not parallel to the base. The longest diameter of the ellipse is called the major axis, and the shortest is the minor axis, both intersecting at the center. In standard form, an ellipse is written as (x − h)^2 / a^2 + (y − k)^2 / b^2 = 1, where (h, k) is the center, and a and b are the distances from the center to the ellipse along the major and minor axes.

Hyperbolas

A hyperbola is a type of conic section formed by the set of all points in a plane where the difference of the distances to two fixed points, called foci, is constant. It consists of two separate, mirror-image curves that open away from each other, either horizontally or vertically. A hyperbola is formed when a plane intersects both nappes (halves) of a double cone at an angle steeper than that of the cone’s side. Its standard equation is (x−h) ^2 /a2−(y−k) ^2 /b2=1 for a horizontally opening hyperbola, where (h, k) is the center, and a and b relate to the shape and asymptotes of the curve.

Conics and System of Nonlinear

A system of nonlinear equations involves two or more equations where at least one equation is nonlinear (not a straight line). In the context of conic sections, this often means equations involving circles, parabolas, ellipses, or hyperbolas.

UNIT 2

Series

A series is the sum of the terms of a sequence, typically involving numbers added together in a specific order and pattern. It can be finite, where a limited number of terms are added, or infinite, where the sum continues indefinitely. Series are often used in mathematics to represent patterns, calculate totals, and analyze functions. Common types include arithmetic series (with a constant difference between terms) and geometric series (with a constant ratio between terms). The study of series is fundamental in areas such as algebra, calculus, and mathematical analysis.

Mathematical Induction

Mathematical induction is a proof technique used to establish the truth of an infinite sequence of mathematical statements or propositions. It involves two key steps: Base case — proving the statement is true for an initial value (usually the smallest integer, like n=1); and Inductive step — assuming the statement is true for some arbitrary integer k, and then proving it holds for k+1. By completing these two steps, mathematical induction demonstrates that the statement is true for all integers greater than or equal to the base case, establishing its validity across the entire sequence. This method is widely used in proving formulas, inequalities, and properties in mathematics.

Binomial Theorem

The Binomial Theorem provides a formula for expanding expressions raised to a power, specifically for any non-negative integer n. It states that for any two terms a and b, the expansion of (a + b)^n can be expressed as a sum of terms in the form (n /k) a ^n – k b ^k, where (n /k) is the binomial coefficient (also read as "n choose k") and represents the number of ways to choose k elements from a set of n elements. The theorem provides a powerful way to compute powers of binomials and is foundational in algebra, combinatorics, and calculus. The expansion involves terms that vary based on the value of k, ranging from 0 to n.
